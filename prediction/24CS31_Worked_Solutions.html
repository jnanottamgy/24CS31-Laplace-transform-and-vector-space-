I.1The 2-mark definition
Q1a and Q2a · 2 marks · one in EACH question, so you get it either way
The question
Any one of these:

•  Define the Laplace transform of a function.
•  Write the Laplace transform of a periodic function.
•  Define a periodic function with an example.
Could it appear another way?
Only three forms have ever been set, and all three are one line.
  • “Define the Laplace transform” — Mar 24 Q2a
  • “Write the Laplace transform of a periodic function” — Mar 24 Q1a
  • “Define a periodic function with an example” — Jun 23 Q1a(i)
One more is overdue: the syllabus lists existence conditions for the Laplace transform and they have never been asked. Learn the one sentence: L{f} exists if f is piecewise continuous on every finite interval and of exponential order, that is |f(t)| ≤ Meat for some constants M and a.
What to write — learn these word for word
1. Laplace transform. For f(t) defined for t ≥ 0,
L{f(t)} = F(s) = ∫0∞e−stf(t) dt
provided the integral converges.

2. Periodic function. f is periodic with period T if f(t+T) = f(t) for all t. Example: sin t, period 2π.

3. Transform of a periodic function of period T:
L{f(t)} = 11 − e−sT∫0Te−stf(t) dt
Watch out
  • Two marks for one line, and it is in both questions — never skip it.
  • Add “provided the integral converges”. It shows you know the transform does not always exist.
  • If asked for an example, give one and state its period.
I.2Given L{sin √t}, show the cos √t result
Unit I · Q1b · 4 marks · 12 minutes
The question
Given  L{sin √t} = √π2s3/2e−1/4s,  show that
L{cos √t√t} = √πs1/2e−1/4s
Could it appear another way?
The sin √t family has been set three different ways. All four papers had one of them.
  • Given the result, derive the other (Jun 23 Q1c, Mar 24 Q1b, 4–7 m) — the version solved here.
  • “Find L{sin √t}” (Apr 23 Q2a, 6 m) — you must derive it from scratch. Expand sin √t as a power series √t − t3/2/3! + t5/2/5! − …, transform term by term using L{tn} = Γ(n+1)/sn+1, and recognise the resulting series as (√π/2s3/2)e−1/4s.
  • “Show that L{sin √t} = …” (Sep 23 Q2a, 6 m) — same as above, but the target is printed, so you can work towards it.
If the derivation version appears and you have not prepared it, answer the other question in the unit.
Formulas you need
  • Transform of a derivative:  L{f′(t)} = s F(s) − f(0)
  • Chain rule:  ddt(sin √t) = cos √t × ddt(√t)
  • ddt(√t) = 12√t
Why this works
You are given a transform and asked for a different one. The trick is to spot that the function you want is just a multiple of the derivative of the function you were given. Differentiating sin √t produces cos √t over 2√t — almost exactly the target, off by a factor of 2. So instead of integrating anything, you use the derivative rule, which turns the problem into one multiplication by s.
Solution — every step
  1. Name the function and note its value at zero.Let  f(t) = sin √t.  Then F(s) = √π2s3/2e−1/4s is what you were given.

    f(0) = sin 0 = 0. Note this now — it is what makes the next step clean.
  2. Differentiate f using the chain rule.
    f′(t) = cos √t × 12√t = cos √t2√t
  3. Spot the relationship to the target.
    cos √t√t = 2 × cos √t2√t = 2 f′(t)
    The function you want is exactly twice the derivative. That is the whole idea of the question.
  4. Take the Laplace transform of both sides.
    L{cos √t√t} = 2 L{f′(t)}
    Apply the derivative rule L{f′} = sF(s) − f(0):
    = 2[s F(s) − f(0)] = 2s F(s) − 0 = 2s F(s)
  5. Substitute the given transform and simplify.
    = 2s × √π2s3/2e−1/4s
    The 2 cancels the 2, and s ÷ s3/2 = s1−3/2 = s−1/2:
    = √πs1/2e−1/4s   ∎
Check your answer
The exponential factor e−1/4s is untouched throughout — it should appear unchanged in your final line. Only the power of s and the constant change. If your exponential has moved, you have differentiated the wrong thing.

The index arithmetic: s1 ÷ s3/2 = s−1/2 = 1/√s = 1/s1/2.  ✓
Watch out
  • Do not try to derive L{sin √t} from scratch. It is given. Deriving it needs a series expansion and will eat your whole exam.
  • You must state f(0) = 0. If you leave the −f(0) term dangling you lose a mark, even though it is zero.
  • The factor is 2, not ½. Differentiating gives a 2 in the denominator, so you multiply by 2 to remove it.
I.3Prove the tn-multiplication rule
Unit I · Q1c or Q2b · 7 marks · 15 minutes
The question
If L{f(t)} = F(s), prove that
L{tnf(t)} = (−1)n dndsn{F(s)}
where n is a positive integer.

This has been set word-for-word in all four past papers.
Could it appear another way?
This proof is always word-for-word identical. But the examiner keeps a small stock of Unit I proofs and rotates them, so the other question may carry one of these instead:
  • L{f(t)/t} = ∫s∞F(u) du (Jun 23 Q2b, 7 m). Proof: write the right side as a double integral, swap the order of integration, do the u-integral to get e−st/t, and you are left with L{f/t}.
  • L{(sinh at)f(t)} = ½[F(s−a) − F(s+a)] (Sep 23 Q2b, 7 m). Proof: sinh at = ½(eat − e−at), then apply the first shifting theorem to each term. Three lines.
  • Both are often followed by “hence find” a specific transform — e.g. L{sin 4t/t} or L[t8 sinh 2t]. Just substitute into the formula you have just proved.
Formulas you need
  • Definition:  F(s) = ∫0∞e−stf(t) dt
  • Differentiation under the integral sign (Leibniz’s rule): you may move dds inside the integral, where it becomes a partial derivative ∂∂s.
  • ∂∂s(e−st) = −t e−st — note t is a constant as far as s is concerned.
Why this works
The whole proof rests on one observation: inside the Laplace integral, differentiating with respect to s pulls down a factor of −t. So each differentiation multiplies the function by −t. Do it n times and you have multiplied by (−t)n = (−1)ntn, which is exactly the statement, rearranged. Everything else is bookkeeping. Write it as a proper induction — that is what the seven marks are for.
Solution — every step
  1. Start from the definition.
    F(s) = ∫0∞e−stf(t) dt
    Every proof in this unit starts on this line. Write it down first, always.
  2. Differentiate both sides with respect to s and move the derivative inside.
    dFds = dds∫0∞e−stf(t) dt = ∫0∞∂∂s\[e−st\]f(t) dt
    This step is Leibniz’s rule. Say the name — examiners look for it.
  3. Do the inner differentiation.
    ∂∂s(e−st) = −t e−st
    so
    dFds = ∫0∞(−t)e−stf(t) dt = −∫0∞e−st\[t f(t)\] dt
  4. Recognise the integral on the right — it is a Laplace transform.
    dFds = −L{t f(t)}   →   L{t f(t)} = −dFds = (−1)1d1Fds1
    This is the result for n = 1 — the base case of the induction.
  5. Assume the result holds for some n.Inductive hypothesis: suppose that for a particular positive integer n,
    L{tnf(t)} = ∫0∞e−sttnf(t) dt = (−1)ndnFdsn
  6. Differentiate the hypothesis once more.Left side — same trick as before, another factor of (−t) comes down:
    dds∫0∞e−sttnf(t)dt = −∫0∞e−sttn+1f(t)dt = −L{tn+1f(t)}
    Right side:
    dds\[(−1)ndnFdsn\] = (−1)ndn+1Fdsn+1
  7. Equate and tidy.
    −L{tn+1f(t)} = (−1)ndn+1Fdsn+1
    Multiply both sides by −1:
    L{tn+1f(t)} = (−1)n+1dn+1Fdsn+1
    This is exactly the statement with n replaced by n+1.
  8. Conclude.The result holds for n = 1, and whenever it holds for n it also holds for n+1. By the principle of mathematical induction it holds for every positive integer n.  ∎
Check your answer
Test the formula on something you know. Take f(t) = 1, so F(s) = 1/s.
The rule with n = 2 predicts L{t2} = (−1)2d2ds2(1/s) = 2s3, and the standard table also gives L{t2} = 2!/s3 = 2/s3.  ✓
Watch out
  • Do not just do the n = 1 case and write “similarly”. The marks are for the induction. State the hypothesis, do the step, state the conclusion.
  • Name Leibniz’s rule when you move the derivative inside the integral.
  • Keep track of the sign. It is (−1)n, not (−1). Each differentiation contributes one factor of −1.

II.1The 2-mark definition
Q3a and Q4a · 2 marks · one in EACH question
The question
•  Define the unit step function and represent it graphically.
•  Define the Dirac-delta function and sketch its graph.
Could it appear another way?
Both are new to the current format (Mar 24 Q3a and Q4a) — the 2-mark slot did not exist in the 2023 papers. They appeared in the same paper, one in each question, so you get one whichever you choose.
  • “Represent graphically”, “sketch its graph” and “draw” all mean the same thing: draw the picture. Half the mark is the sketch.
  • A likely variant is “state the Laplace transform of” the unit step or the delta function. Both are one line, so learn them.
  • Also possible: state the convolution theorem as a 2-marker — write L−1{F(s)G(s)} = ∫0tf(u)g(t−u) du.
What to write — learn these word for word
1. Unit step (Heaviside) function.
u(t−a) = 0  for t < a,    u(t−a) = 1  for t ≥ a
Graph: flat along zero, a vertical jump of height 1 at t = a, then flat at 1. Label the axes and mark t = a.
Its transform: L{u(t−a)} = e−as/s

2. Dirac-delta (unit impulse) function. δ(t−a) is the limit, as ε → 0, of a rectangular pulse of width ε and height 1/ε at t = a. It satisfies
δ(t−a) = 0 for t ≠ a,   and   ∫−∞∞δ(t−a) dt = 1
Graph: a single vertical arrow at t = a, labelled with area 1.
Its transform: L{δ(t−a)} = e−as
Watch out
  • Draw the graph. A definition without the sketch usually scores one out of two.
  • The delta function has zero width and infinite height but area exactly 1. Say the area — that is what distinguishes it from “a very tall spike”.
  • The step is defined with ≥ at t = a. Small detail, easy mark.
II.2Piecewise function via Heaviside, then its Laplace transform
Unit II · Q3c · 7 marks · 20 minutes
The question
Express  f(t) =
t2,0 < t < 2
4t,2 < t < 4
8,t > 4
  in terms of the Heaviside function, and hence find its Laplace transform.
Could it appear another way?
The method never changes — only the pieces do. All four papers set this, with these functions:
  • t2 / 4t / 8 on (0,2), (2,4), (4,∞) — Apr 23 and Mar 24, the version solved here
  • cos t / cos 2t / cos 3t on (0,π), (π,2π), (2π,∞) — Jun 23. Rewriting is easier than it looks: cos(τ+π) = −cos τ.
  • 1 / t / t2 on (0,1], (1,2], (2,∞) — Sep 23
Also watch for the reverse question: some papers give you the Heaviside expression and ask for the inverse transform, or ask you to sketch f(t). Same skills.
Formulas you need
  • The unit step: u(t−a) = 0 for t < a, and 1 for t ≥ a. It is a switch that turns on at t = a.
  • Second shifting theorem:  L{g(t−a) u(t−a)} = e−asG(s)  where G(s) = L{g(t)}
  • L{1} = 1/s,  L{t} = 1/s2,  L{t2} = 2/s3
Why this works
Think of the function as switching. It starts as t2. At t = 2 it must stop being t2 and start being 4t. You achieve that by adding the difference (4t − t2) switched on at t = 2. At t = 4 you add the next difference, (8 − 4t), switched on at t = 4. Each new piece cancels the old one and installs the new one.

The one trap: the second shifting theorem only applies when the thing multiplying u(t−a) is written as a function of (t − a), not of t. So there is always a rewriting step, and that is where most marks are lost.
Solution — every step
  1. Write the function as a sum of switches.General pattern:  f = (first piece) + (second − first)u(t−a1) + (third − second)u(t−a2) + …
    f(t) = t2 + (4t − t2)u(t−2) + (8 − 4t)u(t−4)
    Sanity check. For t between 0 and 2 both switches are off, so f = t2 ✓. For t between 2 and 4 the first switch is on: f = t2 + 4t − t2 = 4t ✓. For t > 4 both are on: f = 4t + 8 − 4t = 8 ✓.
  2. Transform the first term.
    L{t2} = 2/s3
    No switch on this one, so no shifting is needed.
  3. Rewrite the second bracket in terms of (t − 2).Substitute t = τ + 2 (so τ = t − 2):
    4t − t2 = 4(τ+2) − (τ+2)2
    = 4τ + 8 − (τ2 + 4τ + 4) = 4τ + 8 − τ2 − 4τ − 4 = 4 − τ2
    So the term is g(t−2)u(t−2) with g(τ) = 4 − τ2.
  4. Transform it.
    G(s) = L{4 − τ2} = 4s − 2s3
    Apply the second shifting theorem — multiply by e−2s:
    e−2s\(4s − 2s3\)
  5. Rewrite the third bracket in terms of (t − 4).Substitute t = τ + 4:
    8 − 4t = 8 − 4(τ+4) = 8 − 4τ − 16 = −8 − 4τ
    So g(τ) = −8 − 4τ.
  6. Transform it.
    G(s) = −8s − 4s2
    Multiply by e−4s:
    e−4s\(−8s − 4s2\)
  7. Add the three pieces.
    L{f(t)} = 2s3 + e−2s\(4s − 2s3\) + e−4s\(−8s − 4s2\)
Check your answer
Two quick checks.

1. The switch check in step 1. Put a value of t in each interval into your Heaviside expression and confirm you get back the original piece. If that works, the hard half is right.

2. Every exponential must match its switch. The term switched on at t = 2 carries e−2s; the one at t = 4 carries e−4s. If an exponent does not match its switch point, you have mixed up a term.
Watch out
  • The rewriting step is compulsory. Writing L{(4t−t2)u(t−2)} = e−2s L{4t−t2} is wrong and will cost you most of the marks. You must convert to a function of τ = t−2 first.
  • Use differences, not the pieces themselves: it is (4t − t2), not 4t.
  • Expand (τ+2)2 carefully — it is τ2 + 4τ + 4, and the 4τ is what cancels.
II.3Solving ODEs and systems of ODEs by Laplace transforms
Unit II · Q3d or Q4d · 7 marks · 22 minutes
The question
(a) A system.  dxdt − 2y = cos 2t,   dydt + 2x = sin 2t,  given x(0) = 1, y(0) = 0.

(b) A single ODE.  y″ + 4y′ + 3y = e−t,  given y(0) = 0, y′(0) = 0.

(c) A circuit.  A voltage Ee−at is applied at t = 0 to a circuit of inductance L and resistance R. Show the current is ER − aL(e−at − e−Rt/L).
Could it appear another way?
Six different versions have appeared. Every one uses the identical method.
  • Simultaneous, cos/sin forcing — Mar 24 Q4d, solved here.
  • The “particle on a plane curve” version — Apr 23 Q3c and Jun 23 Q4c. Same system, but it then asks you to eliminate t from x(t) and y(t) to show the path is 4x2 + 4xy + 5y2 = 4. Solve as usual, then substitute into the quadratic and use sin2 + cos2 = 1.
  • y″ + 4y′ + 3y = e−t with y(0) = y′(0) = 0 (Jun 23) or y(0) = y′(0) = 1 (Sep 23). Only the initial conditions change — but they change the whole partial-fraction stage, so do both.
  • Third order: y‴ + 2y″ − y′ − 2y = 0 with y(0)=0, y′(0)=0, y″(0)=6 (Apr 23). You need L{y‴} = s3Y − s2y(0) − sy′(0) − y″(0).
  • Another system: dx/dt − y = e−t, dy/dt + x = sin t (Sep 23).
  • The circuit — Mar 24 Q3d, solved here as part (c).
Formulas you need
  • L{y′} = sY − y(0)
  • L{y″} = s2Y − s y(0) − y′(0)
  • L{cos at} = s/(s2+a2),  L{sin at} = a/(s2+a2)
  • L{e−at} = 1/(s+a),  L{t e−at} = 1/(s+a)2
Why this works
A differential equation is hard because it mixes a function with its derivatives. The Laplace transform converts derivatives into multiplication by s, which turns the whole differential equation into an ordinary algebraic one. You solve the algebra for Y(s), then transform back. The initial conditions are not an afterthought — they enter automatically at the transform step, which is why this method is often faster than the classical one.

For a system, both equations transform, and you end up with two linear equations in X(s) and Y(s). Solve them the same way you would solve any pair of simultaneous equations, then invert each.
Solution — every step
  1. (a) Transform both equations.Write X = L{x} and Y = L{y}. Using x(0) = 1 and y(0) = 0:
    sX − 1 − 2Y = ss2+4   →   sX − 2Y = 1 + ss2+4  …(i)
    sY + 2X = 2s2+4   →   2X + sY = 2s2+4  …(ii)
  2. Eliminate Y.Multiply (i) by s and (ii) by 2, then add — the Y terms cancel:
    s×(i):   s2X − 2sY = s + s2s2+4
    2×(ii):   4X + 2sY = 4s2+4
    Add:   (s2+4)X = s + s2 + 4s2+4 = s + 1
    The right-hand side simplified beautifully. So
    X = s + 1s2+4
  3. Invert to get x(t).Split the fraction:
    X = ss2+4 + 1s2+4 = ss2+4 + 12 · 2s2+4
    The second term needed a 2 on top to match the sin formula, so we put in ½ outside to compensate.
    x(t) = cos 2t + 12sin 2t
  4. Back-substitute for Y.From (ii):  sY = 2s2+4 − 2X
    = 2s2+4 − 2(s+1)s2+4 = 2 − 2s − 2s2+4 = −2ss2+4
    Divide by s:
    Y = −2s2+4   →   y(t) = −sin 2t
  5. (b) Transform the single ODE.With y(0) = 0 and y′(0) = 0, both boundary terms vanish:
    s2Y + 4sY + 3Y = 1s+1
    (s2 + 4s + 3)Y = 1s+1   →   (s+1)(s+3)Y = 1s+1
    Y = 1(s+1)2(s+3)
    Note the repeated factor — the (s+1) from the forcing term meets the (s+1) from the characteristic polynomial and gives a square.
  6. Partial fractions.
    1(s+1)2(s+3) = As+1 + B(s+1)2 + Cs+3
    Multiply through by (s+1)2(s+3):
    1 = A(s+1)(s+3) + B(s+3) + C(s+1)2
    Put s = −1:  1 = B(2)  →  B = ½
    Put s = −3:  1 = C(4)  →  C = ¼
    Put s = 0:  1 = 3A + 3(½) + ¼  →  3A = 1 − &sup7;⁄₄ = −¾  →  A = −¼
  7. Invert term by term.
    Y = −14·1s+1 + 12·1(s+1)2 + 14·1s+3
    y(t) = −14e−t + 12t e−t + 14e−3t
  8. (c) The circuit — write the equation first.Kirchhoff’s voltage law for a series LR circuit gives
    Ldidt + Ri = Ee−at,   i(0) = 0
    Transform, using i(0) = 0:
    L(sI) + RI = Es+a  →  I = E(s+a)(Ls+R)
  9. Split and invert.Take L out of the second bracket:  I = E/L(s+a)(s+R/L)

    Use the standard split  1(s+p)(s+q) = 1q−p[1s+p − 1s+q] with p = a, q = R/L. Since q − p = (R−aL)/L, the L’s cancel and the constant becomes E/(R−aL):
    I = ER−aL[1s+a − 1s+R/L]
    i(t) = ER−aL(e−at − e−Rt/L)   ∎
Check your answer
Always substitute back. It is fast and it catches sign errors.

(a) x = cos 2t + ½sin 2t,  y = −sin 2t.
  x′ = −2sin 2t + cos 2t, so x′ − 2y = −2sin 2t + cos 2t + 2sin 2t = cos 2t  ✓
  y′ = −2cos 2t, so y′ + 2x = −2cos 2t + 2cos 2t + sin 2t = sin 2t  ✓
  x(0) = 1 ✓,  y(0) = 0 ✓

(b) y(0) = −¼ + 0 + ¼ = 0 ✓. And y′(0) = ¼ + ½ − ¾ = 0 ✓. Both initial conditions recovered.
Watch out

Watch for the trap version. If the map contains a constant, a square or a product — for instance T(x,y) = (x+1, y) or (x2, y) — it is not linear, and the answer is to produce a counter-example. The quickest one is T(0) ≠ 0.
Formulas you need
A map T is linear if and only if BOTH of these hold for all vectors and all scalars:
  • Additivity:  T(u + v) = T(u) + T(v)
  • Homogeneity:  T(ku) = k T(u)
Why this works
“Linear” means the map does not care whether you combine first and transform, or transform first and combine. The two conditions say exactly that for the two ways of combining vectors: adding them, and scaling them. The proof is always the same shape — take two general vectors with subscripts, work out the left side, work out the right side, and show the two expressions are identical. Do not use numbers; a check with specific vectors proves nothing.
Solution — every step
  1. Set up general vectors.Let  u = (x1, y1)  and  v = (x2, y2), and let k be any scalar. Everything follows from these.
  2. Additivity — work out the left-hand side.First add the vectors:  u + v = (x1+x2,  y1+y2)

    Now apply T to that, using the given rule:
    T(u+v) = ( 3(x1+x2) + (y1+y2),   2(y1+y2),   (x1+x2) − (y1+y2) )
  3. Work out the right-hand side.
    T(u) + T(v) = (3x1+y1,  2y1,  x1−y1) + (3x2+y2,  2y2,  x2−y2)
    Add componentwise:
    = ( (3x1+y1) + (3x2+y2),   2y1+2y2,   (x1−y1) + (x2−y2) )
    Expand the brackets in step 2 and you get exactly this. So additivity holds.
  4. Homogeneity.Scale first:  ku = (kx1, ky1). Then transform:
    T(ku) = (3kx1+ky1,  2ky1,  kx1−ky1)
    Take the common factor k out of every component:
    = k(3x1+y1,  2y1,  x1−y1) = k T(u)
    So homogeneity holds. Both conditions hold, therefore T is linear.
